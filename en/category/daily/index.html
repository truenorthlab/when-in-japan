Everyday life

  • Follow the signals and use crosswalksPedestrians must also obey traffic signals. If there is a crosswalk nearby, cross there. Do not cross the road diagonally except where signs allow it, and do not cross right in front of or right behind a vehicle. Standing in the road in a way that blocks traffic, such as stopping in the roadway to take photos, is also prohibited by law.Law
  • Do not spit or relieve yourself in the streetThe Minor Offenses Act (軽犯罪法) prohibits spitting and urinating or defecating in streets, parks and other places where people gather. Violations can lead to short detention or a petty fine.Law
  • Check whether barbecues are allowed before having one by a river or in a parkWhether you can barbecue on a riverbank or in a park is decided place by place. Some areas do not allow barbecues, and some places prohibit the use of fire. Lighting a fire directly on the ground is prohibited in many places. Before you start, check the guidance from the river office, the local government or the facility manager. On riverbanks, also watch out for sudden rises in the river.Local or facility rule
  • Check the park's rules before using fireworksWhether you can use fireworks in a park depends on the municipality and the park. Even where they are allowed there are conditions such as having water ready and taking your trash home, and aerial or noisy fireworks may be banned. The link is one city's example. Check the park's notices or the municipality's website.Local or facility rule
  • One person's bad manners can shape how all foreign residents are seenRules and manners for daily life differ from country to country. Most bad manners happen because people did not know things are different in Japan, or simply slipped up. Even so, easily noticed behavior such as talking loudly on trains, cutting in line or putting out garbage the wrong way is sometimes covered in the news and on social media. It can turn into views like 'foreigners are...', and all the foreign residents who follow the rules can end up being looked at harshly. This does not mean those views are right. People from any country can make similar mistakes in a place they are not used to. Knowing in advance which behavior often causes problems makes misunderstandings and friction easier to avoid. You can check them in the related items below.Good to know
  • Keep accessible parking spaces free for the people who need themThe wide accessible parking spaces near the entrances of shops and facilities are for people who need extra consideration getting in and out of a car or moving around, such as wheelchair users, people with disabilities, older people and pregnant women. Wheelchair users in particular need to open the car door fully to get in and out, so they cannot use ordinary spaces. Even if one is empty, leave it free if you do not need it.Good to know
  • Do not touch, talk to or feed assistance dogs at workAssistance dogs, meaning guide dogs, service dogs and hearing dogs, are important partners of people with disabilities, not pets. Because they need to focus on their user's instructions, people are asked not to distract them by talking to them, staring at them or touching them. Do not give them food or water either. If the dog is distracted, its user can no longer walk safely.Good to know
  • Cover your mouth and nose when you cough or sneezeWhen you cough or sneeze, cover your mouth and nose with a mask, tissue or handkerchief. If there is no time, use your sleeve or the inside of your elbow. Coughing or sneezing without regard for others is also listed among behaviors people find annoying on trains.Good to know
  • Start with a light bowA light nod or bow when you meet someone or enter or leave a shop comes across as polite. You do not need to memorize detailed rules of bowing. Exchanging greetings with your neighbors day to day makes trouble less likely.Good to know
  • Know what the Help Mark and the Maternity Mark meanThe Help Mark is worn by people who need help or consideration even when it does not show, such as people with artificial limbs or joints, internal disabilities or intractable diseases, or in early pregnancy. The Maternity Mark lets people nearby know that the wearer is pregnant. When you see one, you are asked to act with consideration, such as offering your seat on trains and buses, or speaking to the person if they seem to need help.Good to know
  • For minor illness or injury, go to a nearby clinicMedical institutions in Japan have different roles, and for minor illness or injury you first go to a nearby clinic. If you do not show your My Number health insurance card or similar proof, you pay the full medical costs yourself. If you cannot speak Japanese, you can ask your municipality, an international exchange association or an NPO for help.Good to know
  • Mind your voice and how much space you take up in crowded placesTalking loudly in crowded places such as streets and station passages, or blocking the way in a large group, is seen as a nuisance. Talking itself is not the problem, and a soft voice is fine.Good to know
  • Do not stand on or leave things on tactile pavingTactile paving, the blocks with raised bumps on roads and station floors, guides people with visual impairments, who feel them through the soles of their feet as they walk. Lined blocks show the direction to walk, and dotted blocks mark places that need care, such as before stairs or crosswalks. Bicycles or signs left on or around them, or people standing on them, can cause someone to trip or collide.Good to know
  • Flush your toilet paperWith Japanese flush toilets, use the paper provided and flush it after use. Some countries have a custom of putting used paper in a bin, but the provided paper rarely clogs the toilet here. Where there are dedicated toilet slippers, change into them.Good to know
  • Hold your umbrella so it does not hit peopleOn rainy days, a wet umbrella that touches people or a tip pointed at the person behind you is a nuisance and can be dangerous. Hold a closed umbrella close to your body with the tip pointing down.Good to know
