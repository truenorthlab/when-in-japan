Everyday manners

Manners

One person's bad manners can shape how all foreign residents are seen

Rules and manners for daily life differ from country to country. Most bad manners happen because people did not know things are different in Japan, or simply slipped up. Even so, easily noticed behavior such as talking loudly on trains, cutting in line or putting out garbage the wrong way is sometimes covered in the news and on social media. It can turn into views like 'foreigners are...', and all the foreign residents who follow the rules can end up being looked at harshly. This does not mean those views are right. Knowing in advance which behavior often causes problems makes misunderstandings and friction easier to avoid. You can check them in the related items below.

Machine translation

Do

  • Assume things may be different in Japan, and check first
  • Follow signs and notices when there are any
  • If someone asks you to stop, stop right away

Avoid

  • Assume things work the same as in your own country
  • In a large group, focus only on your friends and stop noticing people around you

Related items

  • Do not talk loudly on trains and busesTalking loudly or making noise on trains and buses disturbs others. When you talk, keep your voice low so you do not bother people around you.Manners
  • Don't take phone calls on trains and busesPassengers are asked to set phones to silent mode and refrain from calls. Near priority seats, some railways ask you to turn your phone off when the car is crowded.Manners
  • Let people off first, then board in lineWhen boarding a train, passengers getting off go first. Wait in line on the platform and board in the order you lined up.Manners
  • Line up at registers and restaurantsPeople line up for everything in Japan: trains, checkouts, restaurants, store openings and more.Manners
  • Do not open products before you payIn Japanese stores, you pay at the register before you open a product. Even if you plan to pay later, opening, eating or drinking something before checkout is seen as bad manners in Japan. It may be no problem in some countries, but people in Japan take it especially seriously.Manners
  • Sort garbage by your municipality's rulesSorting categories and their names differ by area. What worked where you lived before may not apply after you move.Local or facility rule
  • Do not litter or dump garbage illegallyThe Waste Management Act says no one may improperly discard waste, and violations carry penalties. Leaving garbage on a road or in the mountains is one example. Burning garbage outdoors, such as in a yard or an empty lot (野焼き), is also prohibited except in cases the law allows, and carries penalties. Carelessly throwing cans, cigarette butts and other litter on streets or in parks is also prohibited by the Minor Offenses Act (軽犯罪法).Legal rule
  • Do not spit or relieve yourself in the streetThe Minor Offenses Act (軽犯罪法) prohibits spitting and urinating or defecating in streets, parks and other places where people gather. Violations can lead to short detention or a petty fine.Legal rule
  • Keep noise down from night to early morningIn apartment buildings, sound travels easily to the rooms next door, above and below. Take particular care from night until early morning. With musical instruments and audio equipment too, mind the volume and the time of day.Manners
  • Wash before getting in the bathAt public baths and hot springs, wash your body first to keep the shared tub clean.Manners
  • Be considerate of people and places in photosAsk before photographing people you don't know. Don't enter private land or go past fences for a photo, and follow no-photography signs.Manners
  • Pay your taxes and insurance, and ask early if you cannotResident tax applies regardless of nationality, and you also pay National Health Insurance premiums (or tax) and similar charges. When a foreign national applies to extend their period of stay or change their status of residence, their payment record may be checked, and unpaid amounts can affect the review. If you leave them unpaid, you may also face measures such as seizure of property. If paying is difficult, talk to your city office before it becomes overdue. The link is one ward's guide, so check how to pay and where to ask with the city office where you live.Legal rule
  • One person's crime can turn into blame on all foreign residentsA crime committed by one foreign national does not always stay that person's problem. It can spread into blame on all the foreign residents who follow the rules, in words like 'foreigners are...'. This does not mean the blame is right. Following the law protects not only you but also the people you live with. The Ministry of Justice runs a human rights consultation service for people who face discrimination.Manners
Original Japanese

一人のマナー違反が、外国人みんなへの見方につながることがある

生活のルールやマナーは、国や地域によって違います。マナー違反の多くは、日本では違うと知らなかったり、うっかりしたりして起きます。それでも、電車の中の大声や列への割り込み、ごみの出し方のように目につきやすい行動は、ニュースやSNSで取り上げられることもあり、「外国人は…」という見方につながって、ルールを守って暮らす外国人みんなが厳しい目で見られることがあります。その見方が正しいという意味ではありません。よく問題になる行動を先に知っておけば、誤解や摩擦を避けやすくなります。下の関連項目から確かめられます。
